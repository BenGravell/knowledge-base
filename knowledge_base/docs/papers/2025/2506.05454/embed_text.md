## Introduction

There are many emerging machine learning problems where gradients are not accessible or expensive to compute, hindering the application of gradient-based optimization algorithms. For example, fine-tuning large language models (LLMs), particularly at the scale of billions of parameters, faces significant memory bottlenecks, primarily because of the memory-intensive nature of backpropagation. Zeroth-order optimization offers a compelling alternative as it permits gradient estimation via finite differences of loss values. Malladi et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib69)\] reported that zeroth-order methods are capable of fine-tuning a 30-billion-parameter model using a single A100 GPU with 80 GiB memory, whereas gradient-based methods require 8 A100s. In addition to recent advances in fine-tuning LLMs, zeroth-order methods have also found numerous applications in black-box settings \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib17), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib18), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib6)\] and nonsmooth optimization \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib74), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib57), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib47)\] where gradient computation is often infeasible. They have further proven effective in reinforcement learning \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib80), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib68), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib39)\] and distributed learning \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib31), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib93), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib77)\] to reduce computation and communication costs.

It is convenient to understand zeroth-order methods through a surrogate smoothed function defined as ${f_{\lambda}{(x)}}:={{\mathbb{E}}_{u \sim {\mathcal{N}{(0,I_{d})}}}{\lbrack{f{({x + {\lambdau}})}}\rbrack}}$ \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib27), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib74)\]. Since Eq. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S1.E1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")) unbiasedly estimates the gradient of $f_{\lambda}{(x)}$ \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib74)\], i.e., ${{\mathbb{E}}{\lbrack{g_{\lambda}{(x,u)}}\rbrack}} = {{\nabla f_{\lambda}}{(x)}}$, standard convergence analyses directly indicate that $f_{\lambda}{(x)}$ is minimized. Further noting that $f_{\lambda}{(x)}$ is close to $f{(x)}$ when $\lambda$ is small, the convergence of zeroth-order optimization can be established on the original loss $f{(x)}$. For example, when $f{(x)}$ is smooth and convex, zeroth-order methods guarantee that the average of the iterates, ${\overline{x}}_{T}$, after $T$ iterations satisfy ${{\mathbb{E}}{\lbrack{{f{({\overline{x}}_{T})}} - {{\min_{x \in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f}{(x)}}}\rbrack}} \leq {\mathcal{O}{({d/T})}}$; see e.g., \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib74)\].

This implies that zeroth-order optimization implicitly encodes an additive regularizer using the trace of Hessian, which is a widely adopted metric in the literature \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib89), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib88), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib58), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib34), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib24), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib3)\] to differentiate sharp and flat minima \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib40), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib46), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib29), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib75), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib25), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib38)\]. Existing works studying flat minima mostly centered around first-order methods. The work of \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib75), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib105), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib85)\] empirically demonstrated that stochastic gradient descent (SGD) converges to solutions with small expected sharpness ${{\mathbb{E}}_{u \sim {\mathcal{N}{(0,I_{d})}}}{\lbrack{f{({x + {\lambdau}})}}\rbrack}} - {f{(x)}}$ on a variety of vision tasks, which also implies small trace of Hessian according to Eq. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S2.E2 "In 2 Warm-up: Sharpness as Implicit Regularization ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")). It was also shown that SGD with label noise provably decreases trace of Hessian as a regularization term for overparameterized models \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib12), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib22), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib55)\]. Wen et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib89)\] proved that sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib33)\], a method specifically designed for finding flat minima, minimizes trace of Hessian when the batch size is one. Further discussions on the relevance of flat minima, as well as the role of the trace of Hessian can be found in the recent work \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib3)\].

This work formalizes the intuition above and initiates the study of the implicit regularization of zeroth-order optimization with the standard two-point estimator. Despite relying only on function evaluations of $f{(x)}$, we show that zeroth-order optimization converges to flat minima, which are typically characterized using second-order information such as the Hessian matrix ${\nabla^{2}f}{(x)}$. In particular, our contributions are summarized below.

$\bullet$ We define flat minima as the minimizers that achieve the smallest trace of Hessian over the set of all minimizers; see Definition [3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S3.Thmproposition1 "Definition 3.1 (Flat Minima). ‣ 3 Complexity for Finding Flat Minima ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima"). Assuming the function is convex and three times continuously differentiable with Lipschitz-continuous gradient, Hessian, and third derivatives (Assumptions [3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S3.Thmproposition3 "Assumption 3.3 (Smoothness). ‣ 3 Complexity for Finding Flat Minima ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima") and [3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S3.Thmproposition4 "Assumption 3.4 (Convexity). ‣ 3 Complexity for Finding Flat Minima ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")), we prove that zeroth-order optimization with the standard two-point estimator (Algorithm (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 1.1 Related Works ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")) converges to $({\mathcal{O}{({\epsilon/d^{2}})}},\epsilon)$-approximate flat minima, defined in Definition [3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S3.Thmproposition2 "Definition 3.2 (Approximate Flat Minima). ‣ 3 Complexity for Finding Flat Minima ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima"), after $T = {\mathcal{O}{({d^{4}/\epsilon^{2}})}}$ iterations; see Corollary (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#Thmtheorem2 "Corollary 2 (Iteration Complexity for Finding Flat Minima). ‣ 3.1 Convergence Analysis ‣ 3 Complexity for Finding Flat Minima ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima"). While standard convergence analysis directly treats $\lambda^{2}\text{Tr}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{(x)}})}$ as a bias term and controls it by choosing a sufficiently small $\lambda$, we provide a tighter and novel characterization in Section [3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S3.SS1 "3.1 Convergence Analysis ‣ 3 Complexity for Finding Flat Minima ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima") of zeroth-order update dynamics to analyze convergence of ${F{(x)}}:={{f{(x)}} + {{({\lambda^{2}/2})}\text{Tr}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{(x)}})}}}$. This result is of independent and broader interest for advancing the understanding of zeroth-order optimization and naturally extends to analyzing convergence rates of first-order methods such as SAM and SGD on the smoothed loss towards flat minima (Remark [3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S3.Thmremark4 "Remark 3.4. ‣ 3.1 Convergence Analysis ‣ 3 Complexity for Finding Flat Minima ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")).

$\bullet$ We provide empirical evaluations to examine the behavior of the trace of Hessian under zeroth-order optimization across three settings: a test function (Figure (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2 Warm-up: Sharpness as Implicit Regularization ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")), binary classification tasks using overparameterized SVMs and logistic regression (Figure (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")), and language model fine-tuning tasks with RoBERTa \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib61)\] (Figure (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")). Consistent with our theoretical predictions, we observe that the trace of Hessian decreases when using zeroth-order optimization across all these settings. Note that we adopt an estimation of the trace of Hessian on language models for scalability purposes.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to prove that zeroth-order optimization converges to flat minima. Note that all results provided in this paper can be readily extended to zeroth-order optimization with other unbiased estimators of ${\nabla f_{\lambda}}{(x)}$ satisfying ${{\mathbb{E}}{\lbrack{uu^{\top}}\rbrack}} = I_{d}$ such that Eq. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S2.E2 "In 2 Warm-up: Sharpness as Implicit Regularization ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")) holds, including the one-point estimator suggested in \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib32), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib74)\], as well as gradient estimation using random vectors uniformly distributed on the Euclidean sphere \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib81), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib99)\].

### Related Works

Zeroth-Order Optimization. Existing works primarily focused on convergence to a stationary point, while we prove the first result on convergence to flat minima. The development and early advances of zeroth-order optimization can be found in \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib70), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib21)\]. Nesterov and Spokoiny \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib74)\] provided a convergence analysis of zeroth-order optimization across various settings. Their results for nonsmooth convex functions were refined by \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib81)\], while improvements for nonsmooth nonconvex functions were made by \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib57), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib16), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib47)\]. Extensions to the stochastic setting were considered in \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib36)\]. Lower bounds were also provided in \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib90), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib28)\], showing that the dimension dependence in the convergence guarantees of zeroth-order optimization is unavoidable without additional assumptions. Several recent works \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib96), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib69), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib99)\] proved that such dimension dependence can be relaxed to a quantity related to the trace of Hessian. Zeroth-order optimization has been extended to minimax optimization \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib86)\], bilevel optimization \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib1)\], constrained optimization \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib10), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib64)\], and Riemannian optimization \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib52), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib53)\]. It has also been integrated with coordinate descent \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib56)\], conditional gradient descent \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib9)\], SignSGD \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib60)\], and variance reduction techniques \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib59), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib30), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib43)\]. A line of work \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib83), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib66), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib10), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib98), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib79)\] established convergence to second-order stationary points, demonstrating that zeroth-order methods can also escape saddle points. The noisy function evaluation setting was studied in \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib8), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib65), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib4)\], where higher-order smoothness assumptions were used to reduce bias in gradient estimates. The capability of zeroth-order methods for fine-tuning LLMs was first demonstrated by Malladi et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib69)\]. Following this work, several recent studies have introduced various improvements aimed at enhancing runtime efficiency and performance, including momentum \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib102), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib44)\], variance reduction \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib35)\], sparsification \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib37), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib63)\], use of Hessian information \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib103)\], and better sampling strategies \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib19)\].

Sharpness-Aware Minimization. Prior studies of flat minima have mostly centered on first-order methods. Algorithms designed to find flat minima have achieved strong empirical success, including Entropy-SGD \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib15)\], stochastic weight averaging \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib42), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib45)\], and sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib33)\]. Similar methods to SAM were proposed in \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib92), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib104)\], and their efficiency and performance were further enhanced in e.g., \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib49), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib62), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib26), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib106), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib5), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib50)\]. Also inspired by the insights in Eq. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S2.E2 "In 2 Warm-up: Sharpness as Implicit Regularization ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")), Zhang et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib97)\] proposed a gradient-based method that effectively minimizes the smoothed loss. Several recent studies \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib95), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib78), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib94)\] proposed modifying zeroth-order optimization and combining it with principles from SAM to explicitly promote flat minima. In contrast, our work focuses on understanding the implicit regularization effects inherent within the standard zeroth-order optimization. In addition to the trace of Hessian used in this work, other notions of sharpness have also been studied in the literature. One such example is the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian matrix, which has been shown to be implicitly penalized by (S)GD with large learning rates \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib20), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib7), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib87), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib67), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib23), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib2)\] and SAM \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib89), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib11)\]. Li et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib51)\] proved that SAM implicitly promotes balanced solutions on scale-invariant problems. A sharpness measure based on the largest gradient norm in a local neighborhood was proposed in \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib101)\]. Recently, Ahn et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib3)\] provided a formal definition of flat minima and studied the convergence complexity of finding them. A local concept of flat minima was used, defining them as local minima that are also stationary points of the trace of Hessian evaluated at limit points under gradient flow. Two gradient-based algorithms were proposed with convergence guarantees to flat local minima under the assumptions that the loss function is four times continuously differentiable, satisfies the local PL condition, and has a twice Lipschitz limit map under gradient flow. In this work, we adopt a global notion of flat minima and assume convexity of the function to show that zeroth-order optimization with the two-point estimator converges to flat global minima.

However, the qualitative discussion of implicit regularization in this section does not offer insights on the complexity to reach flat minima, which will be the subject of the next section.

## Complexity for Finding Flat Minima

To formally study the convergence complexity of zeroth-order optimization with the two-point estimator towards flat minima, we first define the notion of flat minima that we are interested in.

### Definition 3.1 (Flat Minima)

Suppose that $f{(x)}$ is twice differentiable with a well-defined Hessian matrix. Let $\mathcal{X}^{\ast}:={{\arg{\min_{x \in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f}}{(x)}}$ denote the set of minimizers of $f{(x)}$. We say $x^{\ast}$ is a flat minimum of $f{(x)}$ if $x^{\ast} \in {{\arg{\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}^{\ast}}\text{Tr}}}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{(x)}})}}$. That is, $x^{\ast}$ is a minimizer of $f{(x)}$ that achieves the smallest trace of Hessian among the set of minimizers.

The above definition of flat minima implicitly encodes a constrained optimization problem

This functional constraint is equivalent to requiring $x \in \mathcal{X}^{\ast}$, since it always holds that ${f{(x)}} \geq {{\min_{x \in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f}{(x)}}$. In the literature on functional constrained optimization problems ${\min_{x \in \mathcal{C}}\psi_{0}}{(x)}$ with the constrained set $\mathcal{C}:=\left. \{{x \in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}} \middle| {{\psi_{1}{(x)}} \leq 0}\} \right.$, a common objective is to find $\epsilon$-approximate solutions $\hat{x}$ satisfying ${{\psi_{0}{(\hat{x})}} - {{\min_{x \in \mathcal{C}}\psi_{0}}{(x)}}} \leq \epsilon$ and ${\psi_{1}{(\hat{x})}} \leq \epsilon$ \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib71), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib13), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib100)\]. Motivated from this connection to constrained optimization, we define approximate flat minima in the following.

### Definition 3.2 (Approximate Flat Minima)

Suppose that $f{(x)}$ is twice differentiable with a well-defined Hessian matrix. Let $\mathcal{X}^{\ast}:={{\arg{\min_{x \in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f}}{(x)}}$ denote the set of minimizers of $f{(x)}$. For ${\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}} > 0$, we say $\hat{x}$ is an $(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})$-approximate flat minimum of $f{(x)}$ if

Ahn et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib3)\] defined flat minima as local minima that are also stationary points of the trace of Hessian evaluated at limit points under gradient flow. Since the trace of Hessian is highly nonconvex, such a local definition does not necessarily correspond to minima with the lowest trace of Hessian. In this work, we adopt a global notion of flat minima and prove that zeroth-order optimization converges to them. The following assumptions are required to guarantee convergence to approximate flat minima.

### Assumption 3.3 (Smoothness)

We assume the function $f{(x)}$ is three times continuously differentiable and satisfies that $(a)$ $f{(x)}$ is $L_{1}$-smooth; $(b)$ $f{(x)}$ is second-order smooth with $L_{2} > 0$, which implies that all third-order partial derivatives are bounded: ${|{\partial^{3}{{f{(x)}}/{\partial{x_{i}{\partial{x_{j}{\partial x_{k}}}}}}}}|} \leq L_{2}$, ${{\forall i},j,k} \in {\lbrack d\rbrack}$ and ${\forall x} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}$; and, $(c)$ $f{(x)}$ is third-order smooth with $L_{3} > 0$, which implies that ${{\forall x},y} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}$,

where ${{\nabla^{3}f}{(x)}{\lbrack{y - x}\rbrack}^{3}} = {\sum_{{i,j,k} \in {\lbrack d\rbrack}}{{({\partial^{3}{{f{(x)}}/{\partial{x_{i}{\partial{x_{j}{\partial x_{k}}}}}}}})}{({y_{i} - x_{i}})}{({y_{j} - x_{j}})}{({y_{k} - x_{k}})}}}$.

Since the characterization of flat minima already involves second-order information of $f{(x)}$, it is natural to require assumptions on higher-order information to establish convergence guarantees. Higher-order smoothness assumptions have been widely used to establish fast convergence rates \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib8), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib72)\], guarantee convergence to second-order stationary points \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib30), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib79)\], analyze implicit regularization \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib7), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib89)\], and study the complexity of finding flat minima \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib3)\]. We emphasize that these assumptions on higher-order information are used solely for convergence analysis; Algorithm (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 1.1 Related Works ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima") requires only zeroth-order information. In order to prove global convergence, we also need the following convexity assumption. Although $f{(x)}$ is convex, $\text{Tr}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{(x)}})}$ is in general nonconvex. Seeking flat minima with lowest $\text{Tr}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{(x)}})}$ in the set of minimizers $\mathcal{X}^{\ast}$ is therefore a challenging task.

### Assumption 3.4 (Convexity)

The function $f{(x)}$ is convex on ${\mathbb{R}}^{d}$, and thus ${\text{Tr}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{(x)}})}} \geq 0$.

### Remark 3.3

We focus on the convex setting in this work, but the analysis extends to cases where the objective is locally convex in a neighborhood. When initialized in this region, zeroth-order optimization identifies local minima with the smallest trace of Hessian among all local minimizers in the neighborhood. When $f{(x)}$ is generally nonconvex, the current definition of flat minima is not theoretically tractable without additional assumptions. We leave for future work a detailed study on the definition of computationally feasible flat minima and the assumptions required to understand the complexity of finding them in the nonconvex setting \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib2)\].

### Remark 3.4

Our proof framework can be extended to understand the complexity of first-order methods towards flat minima. For example, a gradient-based method, $x_{t + 1}\leftarrow{x_{t} - {\eta{\nabla f}{({x_{t} + {\lambdau_{t}}})}}}$ with $u_{t} \sim {\mathcal{N}{(0,I_{d})}}$, that uses a gradient evaluated at the perturbed point as the descent direction also minimizes the smoothed loss $f_{\lambda}{(x)}$ in the expectation. By upper bounding ${\mathbb{E}}{\parallel{{\nabla f}{({x + {\lambdau}})}}\parallel}^{2}$ with the term ${\parallel{{\nabla F}{(x)}}\parallel}^{2}$, convergence guarantees on $F{(x)}$ and thus to flat minima can be established. The same framework provides new insights on how SAM can be analyzed as well. Our primary focus is on zeroth-order methods, and extensions to first-order methods are deferred to future.

In classical optimization theory \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib74), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib28)\], the convergence rates of zeroth-order methods scale with the dimension $d$, limiting their applicability to problems with large $d$ such as language model fine-tuning. Assuming ${\text{Tr}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{(x)}})}} \ll {dL_{1}}$ when $f{(x)}$ is $L_{1}$-smooth, zeroth-order optimization achieves dimension-independent rates and remains effective even in high-dimensional settings. Our experimental results show that the trace of Hessian decreases and attains values much smaller than the actual dimension, thereby supporting the assumption made in prior work to explain the empirical success of zeroth-order optimization.

## Conclusion

Motivated by the observation that zeroth-order optimization with the two-point estimator (Algorithm (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 1.1 Related Works ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")) inherently minimizes $\text{Tr}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{(x)}})}$, we initiate a formal study of this implicit regularization. Specifically, we analyze its convergence to flat minima, defined as the ones with the lowest trace of Hessian among all minimizers. For convex and sufficiently smooth (Assumptions [3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S3.Thmproposition3 "Assumption 3.3 (Smoothness). ‣ 3 Complexity for Finding Flat Minima ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")) functions, we prove that Algorithm (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 1.1 Related Works ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima") guarantees $({\mathcal{O}{({\epsilon/d^{2}})}},\epsilon)$-approximate flat minima (Definition [3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#S3.Thmproposition2 "Definition 3.2 (Approximate Flat Minima). ‣ 3 Complexity for Finding Flat Minima ‣ Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima")) after $T = {\mathcal{O}{({d^{4}/\epsilon^{2}})}}$ iterations. This is the first work showing that zeroth-order optimization converges to flat minima. Experiments on binary classification tasks using SVMs and logistic regression, as well as language model fine-tuning tasks on RoBERTa support our theoretical findings.

Theoretical and empirical performance of zeroth-order methods is often limited by the high variance in gradient estimation. A promising direction is to combine zeroth-order and first-order methods to leverage the strengths of both \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib69), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib102), (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05454v2#bib.bib54)\]. We only examine zeroth-order optimization using the standard two-point estimator. Exploring whether the convergence complexity can be further improved with possible modifications and additional algorithmic designs remains an interesting line of work. The current theoretical results require convexity and higher-order smoothness assumptions of the function. Investigation into nonconvex functions with relaxed assumptions is left for future work.
