## Introduction

In modern machine learning applications, the training loss function $f:{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}}$ to be optimized often has a continuum of local/global minima, and the central question is which minima lead to good prediction performance. Among many different properties for minima, "flatness" of minima has been a promising candidate extensively studied in the literature (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib35); Keskar et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib42); Dinh et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib21); Dziugaite and Roy, (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib25); Neyshabur et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib49); Sagun et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib52); Yao et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib59); Chaudhari et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib13); He et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib34); Mulayoff and Michaeli, (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib48); Tsuzuku et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib53); Xie et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib58)).

Recently, there has been a resurgence of interest in flat minima due to various advances in both empirical and theoretical domains. Motivated by the extensive research on flat minima, this work undertakes a formal study that

delineates a clear definition for flat minima, and

studies the upper complexity bounds of finding them.

We begin by emphasizing the significance of flat minima, based on recent advancements in the field.

### Why Flat Minima?

Several recent optimization methods that are explicitly designed to find flat minima have achieved substantial empirical success (Chaudhari et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib13); Izmailov et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib36); Foret et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib28); Wu et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib57); Zheng et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib61); Norton and Royset, (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib50); Kaddour et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib39)). One notable example is sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) (Foret et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib28)), which has shown significant improvements in prediction performance of deep neural network models for image classification problems (Foret et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib28)) and language processing problems (Bahri et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib7)). Furthermore, research by Liu et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib45)) indicates that for language model pretraining, the flatness of minima serves as a more reliable predictor of model efficacy than the pretraining loss itself, particularly when the loss approaches its minimum values.

Complementing the empirical evidence, recent theoretical research underscores the importance of flat minima as a desirable attribute for optimization. Key insights include:

*Provable generalization of flat minima.* For overparameterized models, research by Ding et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib20)) demonstrates that flat minima correspond to the true solutions in low-rank matrix recovery tasks, such as matrix/bilinear sensing, robust PCA, matrix completion, and regression with a single hidden layer neural network, leading to better generalization. This is further extended by Gatmiry et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib30)) to deep linear networks learned from linear measurements. In other words, in a range of nonconvex problems with multiple minima, flat minima yield superior predictive performance.

*Benifits of flat minima in pretraining.* Along with the empirical validations, Liu et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib45)) prove that in simplified masked language models, flat minima correlate with the most generalizable solutions.

*Inductive bias of algorithms towards flat minima.* It has been proved that various practical optimization algorithms inherently favor flat minima. This includes stochastic gradient descent (SGD) (Blanc et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib9); Wang et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib54); Damian et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib17); Li et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib44); Liu et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib45)), gradient descent (GD) with large learning rates (Arora et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib6); Damian et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib18); Ahn et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib2)), sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) (Bartlett et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib8); Wen et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib55); Compagnoni et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib14); Dai et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib16)), and a communication-efficient variant of SGD (Gu et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib33)). The practical success of these algorithms indicates that flatter minima might be linked to better generalization properties.

Motivated by such recent advances, the main goal of this work is to initiate a formal study of the behavior of algorithms for finding flat minima, especially an understanding of their upper complexity bounds.

### Overview of Our Main Results

In this work, we formulate a particular notion of flatness for minima and design efficient algorithms for finding them. We adopt the trace of Hessian of the loss ${tr}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{({\mathbf{x}})}})}$ as a measure of "flatness," where lower values of the trace imply flatter regions within the loss landscape. The reasons governing this choice are many, especially its deep relevance across a rich variety of research, as summarized in [Subsection 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#S2.SS1 "2.1 Measure of Flatness ‣ 2 Formulating Flat Minima ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations"). With this metric, we characterize a flat minimum as a local minimum where any local enhancement in flatness would result in an increased cost, effectively delineating regions where the model is both stable and efficient in terms of performance. More formally, we define the notion of $(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})$-flat minima in [Definition 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#Thmdefinition3 "Definition 3 ((ϵ,ϵ')-flat local minima). ‣ 2.2 Formal Definition of Flat Minima ‣ 2 Formulating Flat Minima ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations"). See [Section 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#S2 "2 Formulating Flat Minima ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations") for precise details.

Given the notion of flat minima, the main goal of this work is to design algorithms that find an approximate flat minimum efficiently. At first glance, the goal of finding a flat minimum might seem computationally expensive because minimizing ${tr}{({\nabla^{2}f})}$ would require information about second or higher derivatives. Notably, this work demonstrates that one can reach a flat minimum using only first derivatives (gradients).

In [Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#S3 "3 Randomly Smoothed Perturbation Escapes Sharp Minima ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations"), we present a gradient-based algorithm called the randomly smoothed perturbation algorithm ([Algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Main Result ‣ 3 Randomly Smoothed Perturbation Escapes Sharp Minima ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations")) which finds a $(\epsilon,\sqrt{\epsilon})$-flat minimum within $\mathcal{O}\left( \epsilon^{- 3} \right)$ iterations for general costs without structure ([Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1. ‣ 3.1 Main Result ‣ 3 Randomly Smoothed Perturbation Escapes Sharp Minima ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations")). The main component of the algorithm is to use gradients computed from randomly perturbed iterates to estimate a direction that leads to flatter minima.

In [Section 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#S4 "4 Faster Escape with Sharpness-Aware Perturbation ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations"), we consider the setting where $f$ is the training loss over a training data set and the initialization is near the set of global minima, motivated by overparametrized models in practice (Zhang et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib60)). In such a setting, we present another gradient-based algorithm called the sharpness-aware perturbation algorithm ([Algorithm 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 4.1 Main Result ‣ 4 Faster Escape with Sharpness-Aware Perturbation ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations")), inspired by sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) (Foret et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib28)). We show that this algorithm finds a $(\epsilon,\sqrt{\epsilon})$-flat minimum within $\mathcal{O}\left( {d^{- 1}\epsilon^{- 2}{({1 \vee \frac{1}{d^{3}\epsilon}})}} \right)$ iterations ([Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 2. ‣ 4.1 Main Result ‣ 4 Faster Escape with Sharpness-Aware Perturbation ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations")) -- here $d$ denotes the dimension of the domain. This demonstrates that a practical algorithm like SAM can find flat minima much faster than the randomly smoothed perturabtion algorithm in high dimensional settings.

## Formulating Flat Minima

In this section, we formally define the notion of flat minima.

### Measure of Flatness

Within the literature reviewed in [Subsection 1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#S1.SS1 "1.1 Why Flat Minima? ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations"), a recurring metric for evaluating "flatness" in loss landscapes is the trace of the Hessian matrix of the loss function ${tr}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{({\mathbf{x}})}})}$. This metric intuitively reflects the curvature of the loss landscape around minima, where the Hessian matrix ${\nabla^{2}f}{({\mathbf{x}})}$ is expected to be positive semi-definite. Consequently, lower values of the trace indicate regions where the loss landscape is flatter. For simplicity, we will refer to this metric as *the trace of Hessian*. Key insights from recent research include:

Figure 1: Figure from Liu et al.. They pretrain language models for probabilistic context-free grammar with different optimization methods, and compare their downstream accuracy. As shown in the plot, the trace of Hessian is a better indicator of the performance than the pretraining loss itself.

Figure 2: Figure from Damian et al.. For training ResNet-18 on CIFAR 10, they measure the trace of Hessian across the iterates of SGD with label noise and have observed an inspiring relation between tr(∇2f(xt)) and prediction performance.

*Overparameterized low-rank matrix recovery.* In this domain, the trace of Hessian has been identified as the *correct notion of flatness*. Ding et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib20)) show that the most desirable minima, which correspond to ground truth solutions, are those with the lowest trace of Hessian values. This principle is also applicable to the analysis of deep linear networks, as highlighted by Gatmiry et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib30)), where the same measure plays a pivotal role.

*Language model pretraining.* The importance of the trace of Hessian extends to language model pretraining, as demonstrated by Liu et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib45)). More specifically, Liu et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib45)) conduct an insightful experiment (see [Figure 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.1 Measure of Flatness ‣ 2 Formulating Flat Minima ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations")) that demonstrates the effectiveness of the trace of Hessian as a good measure of model performance. This observation is backed by their theoretical results for simple language models.

*Model output stability.* Furthermore, the work (Ma and Ying, (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib47)) links the trace of Hessian to the stability of model outputs in deep neural networks relative to input data variations. This relationship underscores the significance of the trace of Hessian in improving model generalization and enhancing adversarial robustness.

*Practical optimization algorithms.* Lastly, various practical optimization algorithms are shown to be inherently biased toward achieving lower values of the trace of Hessian. This includes SGD with label noise, as discussed in works by Blanc et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib9)); Damian et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib17)); Li et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib44)), and without label noise for the language modeling pretraining (Liu et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib45)). In particular, Damian et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib17)) conduct an inspiring experiment showing a strong correlation between the trace of Hessian and the prediction performance of models (see [Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.1 Measure of Flatness ‣ 2 Formulating Flat Minima ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations")). Additionally, stochastic SAM is proven to prefer lower trace of Hessian values (Wen et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib55)).

### Remark 1 (Other notions of flatness?)

Perhaps, another popular notion of flat minima in the literature is the maximum eigenvalue of Hessian $\lambda_{\max}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{(\mathbf{x})}})}$. However, recent empirical works (Kaur et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib41); Andriushchenko et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib4)) have shown that the maximum eigenvalue of Hessian has limited correlation with the goodness of models (*e.g.*, generalization). On the other hand, as we detailed above, the trace of Hessian has been consistently brought up as a promising candidate, both theoretically and empirically. Hence, we adopt the trace of Hessian as the measure of flatness throughout.

### Formal Definition of Flat Minima

Motivated by the previous works discussed above, we adopt the trace of Hessian as the measure of flatness. Specifically, we consider the (normalized) trace of Hessian ${{{tr}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{({\mathbf{x}})}})}}/{{tr}{(I_{d})}}} = {{{tr}{({{\nabla^{2}f}{({\mathbf{x}})}})}}/d}$. Here we use the normalization to match the scale of flatness with the loss. For simplicity, we henceforth use the following notation:

The reason we consider the normalized trace is to match its scale with that of loss $f{({\mathbf{x}})}$: the trace is in general the sum of $d$ second derivatives, so it's scale is $d$ times of that of $f{({\mathbf{x}})}$. Also, the normalization can be potentially beneficial in practice where models have different sizes. Larger models would typically have a higher trace of Hessian due to having more parameters, and the normalization could put them on the same scale.

Given this choice, our notion of flat minima at a high level is *a local minimum (of $f$) for which one cannot locally decrease $\overline{\mathsf{t}\mathsf{r}}$ without increasing the cost $f$.* In particular, this concept becomes nontrivial when the set of local minima is connected (or locally forms a manifold), which is indeed the case for the over-parametrized neural networks, as shown empirically in (Draxler et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib22); Garipov et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib29)) and theoretically in (Cooper, (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib15)).

One straightforward way to define a (locally) flat minimum is the following: a local minimum which is also a local minimum of $\overline{\mathsf{t}\mathsf{r}}$. However, this definition is not well-defined as the set of local minima of $f$ can be disjoint from that of $\overline{\mathsf{t}\mathsf{r}}$ as shown in the following example.

## Randomly Smoothed Perturbation Escapes Sharp Minima

In this section, we present a gradient-based algorithm for finding an approximate flat minimum. We first discuss the setting for our analysis.

In order for our notion of flat minima ([Definition 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#Thmdefinition3 "Definition 3 ((ϵ,ϵ')-flat local minima). ‣ 2.2 Formal Definition of Flat Minima ‣ 2 Formulating Flat Minima ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations")) to be well defined, we assume that the loss function is four times continuously differentiable near the local minima set $\mathcal{X}^{\star}$. More formally, we make the following assumption about the loss function.

### Assumption 1 (Loss near minima)

There exists $\zeta > 0$ such that within $\zeta$-neighborhood of the set of local minima $\mathcal{X}^{\star}$, the following properties hold:

$f$ is four-times continuously differentiable.

We also discuss a preliminary step for our analysis. Since the question of finding candidates for approximate local minima (or second order stationary points) is well-studied, thanks to the vast literature on the topic over the last decade (Ge et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib31); Agarwal et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib1); Carmon et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib10); Fang et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib26); Jin et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib38)), we do not further explore it, but single out the question of seeking flatness by assuming that the initial iterate ${\mathbf{x}}_{0}$ is already close to the set of local minima $\mathcal{X}^{\star}$. ## Experiments

We run experiments based on training ResNet-18 on the dataset to test the ability of proposed algorithms to escape sharp global minima. Following (Damian et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib17)), the algorithms are initialized at a point corresponding to a sharp global minimizer that achieve poor test accuracy. Crucially, we choose this setting because (Damian et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib17), Figure 1) verify that test accuracy is inversely correlated with the trace of Hessian (see [Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.1 Measure of Flatness ‣ 2 Formulating Flat Minima ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations")). This bad global minimizer, due to (Liu et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib46)), achieves $100\%$ training accuracy, but only $48\%$ test accuracy. We choose the constant learning rate of $\eta = 0.001$, which is small enough such that SGD baseline without any perturbation does not escape.

We discuss the results one by one. First of all, we highlight that the training accuracy stays at $100\%$ for all algorithms.

Comparison between two methods. In the left plot of [Figure 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.2 Proof Sketch of Theorem 2 ‣ 4 Faster Escape with Sharpness-Aware Perturbation ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations"), we compare the performance of Randomly Smoothed Perturbation ("RS") and Sharpness-Aware Perturbation ("SA"). We choose the batch size of $128$ for both methods. Consistent with our theory, one can see that SA is more effective in escaping sharp minima even with a smaller perturbation radius $\rho$.

Different batch sizes. Our theory suggests that batch size $1$ should be effective in escaping sharp minima. We verify this in the right plot of [Figure 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.2 Proof Sketch of Theorem 2 ‣ 4 Faster Escape with Sharpness-Aware Perturbation ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations") by choosing the batch size to be $B = {1,64,128}$. We do see that the case of $B = 1$ is quite effective in escaping sharp minima.

## Related Work and Future Work

The last decade has seen a great success in theoretical studies on the question of finding (approximate) stationary points (Ghadimi and Lan, (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib32); Ge et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib31); Agarwal et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib1); Daneshmand et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib19); Carmon et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib10); Fang et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib26); Allen-Zhu, (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib3); Zhou and Gu, (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib62); Jin et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib38)). This work extends this line of research to a new notion of stationary point, namely an approximate flat minima. We believe that further studies on defining/refining practical notions of flat minima and designing efficient algorithms for them would lead to better understanding of practical nonconvex optimization for machine learning. In the same spirit, we believe that characterizing lower bounds would be of great importance, similar to the ones for the stationary points (Carmon et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib11); Drori and Shamir, (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib23); Carmon et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib12); Arjevani et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib5)).

Another important direction is to further investigate the effectiveness of the flatness. As we discussed in (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#Thmremark1 "Remark 1 (Other notions of flatness?). ‣ 2.1 Measure of Flatness ‣ 2 Formulating Flat Minima ‣ How to Escape Sharp Minima with Random Perturbations"), recent results have shown that other notions of flatness are not always a good indicator of model efficacy (Andriushchenko et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib4); Wen et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib56)). It would be interesting to understand the precise role of flatness, given that we have a lot of evidence of its success. Moreover, studying other notions of flatness, such as the "effective size of basin" as considered in (Kleinberg et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib43); Feng et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib27)), or the constrained settings (*e.g.*, (Feng et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib27))), and exploring the algorithmic questions there would be also interesting future directions.

Based on our analysis, we suspect that replacing the full-batch gradients with the stochastic gradients in our proposed algorithms also leads to an efficient algorithm, with a more careful stochastic analysis. Moreover, we suspect that our results have sub-optimal dependence on the error probability $\delta$, and a more advanced analysis will likely leads to a better dependence (Jin et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2305.15659v3#bib.bib38)). Lastly, based on our experiments, it seems that a smaller batch size has the same effect as using a larger perturbation radius $\rho$. Whether one can capture this effect theoretically would be also an intriguing direction. However, the main scope of this work is to initiate the study of complexity of finding flat minima, and we leave all of this to future works.
