## Introduction

Second-order methods are renowned for their faster convergence compared to first-order methods, and the capability to find second-order stationary points, see Cartis et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib44 "Evaluation complexity of algorithms for nonconvex optimization: theory, computation and perspectives")\], Nocedal and Wright \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib36 "Numerical optimization")\], Nesterov \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib42 "Lectures on convex optimization")\], Carmon et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib12 "Lower bounds for finding stationary points I"), (https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib66 "Lower bounds for finding stationary points ii: first-order methods")\]. Among these methods, the trust-region (TR) method \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib47 "Trust region methods")\] stands out as a representative approach due to its robustness in nonconvex problems and extraordinary numerical performance. Many linear or nonlinear programming solvers are using the trust-region method as an important building block, to name a few, Knitro \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib57 "Knitro: an integrated package for nonlinear optimization")\], IPOPT \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib67 "On the implementation of an interior-point filter line-search algorithm for large-scale nonlinear programming")\], and PDFO \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib71 "PDFO: a cross-platform package for powell’s derivative-free optimization solvers")\], etc. Also, in the machine learning field, it inspires the well-known trust-region policy optimization \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib13 "Trust region policy optimization"), (https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib63 "Model-ensemble trust-region policy optimization")\].

Despite its success in practice as mentioned above, the theoretical development of the TR method is somehow incomplete.

For instance, results in the early literature \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib2 "Newton’s Method with a Model Trust Region Modification"), (https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib3 "A Family of Trust-Region-Based Algorithms for Unconstrained Minimization with Strong Global Convergence Properties"), (https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib47 "Trust region methods")\] only established (asymptotic) local convergence to $\epsilon$-approximate second-order stationary points ($\epsilon$-SOSP). These results also implied that TR has a nonasymptotic iteration complexity of $O\left( \epsilon^{- 2} \right)$ for $\epsilon$-SOSPs. However, such convergence rate is no better than first-order methods \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib42 "Lectures on convex optimization")\] for $\epsilon$-approximate first-order stationary points ($\epsilon$-FOSP), and an improved convergence rate is not aware for convex functions.

This method was originally proposed by Griewank \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib72 "The modification of newton’s method for unconstrained optimization by bounding cubic terms")\] and further analyzed by Nesterov and Polyak \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib43 "Cubic regularization of newton method and its global performance")\]. Later, Cartis et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib45 "Adaptive cubic regularisation methods for unconstrained optimization. part i: motivation, convergence and numerical results"), (https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib46 "Adaptive cubic regularisation methods for unconstrained optimization. part ii: worst-case function-and derivative-evaluation complexity")\] proposed the adaptive version of this method. It shows a convergence rate of $O\left( \epsilon^{- {3/2}} \right)$ to find $\epsilon$-SOSPs for nonconvex functions and $O\left( \epsilon^{- {1/2}} \right)$ for convex case simultaneously.

However, classical TR exhibits a clear gap to these convergence properties. This arises possibly due to two aspects: first, the classic TR method does not fully exploit the curvature information of Hessian; second, the ratio test ([1.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#S1.E2 "Equation 1.2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New AnalysesAccepted by Journal of Scientific Computing, 2026.")) does not explicitly quantify the desired decrease for the TR steps \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib39 "A trust region algorithm with a worst-case iteration complexity of ⁢O(ϵ-/32) for nonconvex optimization")\]; both are crucial in modern complexity analysis.

Therefore, a plethora of TR variants (e.g., \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib39 "A trust region algorithm with a worst-case iteration complexity of ⁢O(ϵ-/32) for nonconvex optimization"), (https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib41 "Trust-region newton-cg with strong second-order complexity guarantees for nonconvex optimization"), (https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib40 "A consistently adaptive trust-region method"), (https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib33 "Linear and Nonlinear Programming")\]) have been proposed to improve iteration complexity over the years. The fixed-radius variant by Luenberger and Ye \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib33 "Linear and Nonlinear Programming")\] achieves a complexity of $O\left( \epsilon^{- {3/2}} \right)$ for finding $\epsilon$-SOSPs by controlling the stepsize proportionally to the tolerance $\epsilon^{1/2}$. However, this variant tends to be conservative for practical applications. By designing a contraction and expansion mechanism, Curtis et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib39 "A trust region algorithm with a worst-case iteration complexity of ⁢O(ϵ-/32) for nonconvex optimization")\] introduced the first adaptive trust-region method (TRACE) matching the $\overset{\sim}{O}\left( \epsilon^{- {3/2}} \right)$ complexity for finding $\epsilon$-SOSPs, where $\overset{\sim}{O}$ means there is a logarithmic term hidden in the complexity. Later, Curtis et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib41 "Trust-region newton-cg with strong second-order complexity guarantees for nonconvex optimization")\] proposed another simplified variant of \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib39 "A trust region algorithm with a worst-case iteration complexity of ⁢O(ϵ-/32) for nonconvex optimization")\] while retaining the same dependency on $\epsilon$. A notable recent trust-region method introduced by Hamad and Hinder \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib40 "A consistently adaptive trust-region method")\] achieves $O\left( \epsilon^{- {3/2}} \right)$ complexity and improves the complexity's dependency over Lipschitz constants by putting together the upper and lower bounds on the stepsizes. However, their algorithm only converges to an $\epsilon$-FOSP. Generally, these novel variants transcend the classic ones to bring a per-step sufficient decrease

As a result, $O\left( \epsilon^{- {3/2}} \right)$ rate of convergence can be achieved. However, for TR-type algorithms, such sufficient decrease ([1.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#S1.E4 "Equation 1.4 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New AnalysesAccepted by Journal of Scientific Computing, 2026.")) is highly nontrivial, due to its heavy dependence on the posterior dual multiplier of the subproblem ([1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#S1.E1 "Equation 1.1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New AnalysesAccepted by Journal of Scientific Computing, 2026.")), which is difficult to control in advance. As a price to overcome this challenge, these algorithms rely on complicated feedback to adjust the dual multiplier and may need additional assumptions. Moreover, these TR-type methods are initially designed for nonconvex optimization, and the associated convergence analysis in convex optimization is largely missing. However, even in many nonconvex problems, the objective function quickly turns weakly convex near some stationary points, which makes the study of TR-type methods in convex optimization equally important.

There is a clear mismatch between the theoretical guarantees and conciseness of implementation, thus calling for a simpler trust-region method with state-of-the-art complexity rates.

The feature of the subproblem above is incorporating gradient regularization in the local quadratic model and setting the trust-region radius proportional to the square root of the gradient norm. As a result, the $d_{k}$ generated by ([1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#S1.E5 "Equation 1.5 ‣ Contributions. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New AnalysesAccepted by Journal of Scientific Computing, 2026.")) exhibits a powerful feature referred to as Function-or-Stationarity-Decrease (FOSD, [Property 2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#S2.Thmproperty2 "Property 2.2 (Function-or-Stationarity-Decrease). ‣ 2.1 Overview of the Method ‣ 2 The Universal Trust-Region Method ‣ Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New AnalysesAccepted by Journal of Scientific Computing, 2026.")), i.e., after one successful update, either the function value decreases sufficiently or the gradient norm shrinks linearly. Different from ([1.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#S1.E4 "Equation 1.4 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New AnalysesAccepted by Journal of Scientific Computing, 2026.")), this desirable property enables a concise and clean algorithm which does not need sophisticated inner loop to control the posterior multiplier $\lambda_{k}$, and the associated complexity results have the state-of-the-art dependency over both the tolerance $\epsilon$ and the Lipschitz constant, requiring only the most standard assumptions.

More specifically, with explicit information on the Hessian Lipschitz constant, we propose a simple strategy to choose parameters $\left( \sigma_{k},r_{k} \right)$ ([Strategy 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#S2.Thmstrategy1 "Strategy 2.1 (The Simple Strategy). ‣ Basic Principle of Choosing (𝜎_𝑘,𝑟_𝑘) ‣ 2.2 Basic Properties of the Method ‣ 2 The Universal Trust-Region Method ‣ Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New AnalysesAccepted by Journal of Scientific Computing, 2026.")), and UTR converges to an $\epsilon$-FOSP within $\overset{\sim}{O}\left( \epsilon^{- {3/2}} \right)$ iterations. Surprisingly, we also find that UTR exhibits an improved $O\left( \epsilon^{- {1/2}} \right)$ complexity when the objective function is convex. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first nonasymptotic analysis of TR-type methods in convex optimization.

Under the guidance of the simple strategy, we develop an adaptive approach ([Strategy 4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#S4.Thmstrategy1 "Strategy 4.1 (The Strategy for Second-order Stationary Points). ‣ 4.1 The Adaptive Framework ‣ 4 The Adaptive Universal Trust-Region Method ‣ Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New AnalysesAccepted by Journal of Scientific Computing, 2026.")) that is more practical without relying on the Lipschitz constant. By fully exploiting the curvature information of the Hessian, this adaptive approach provides a convergence guarantee with an iteration complexity of $\overset{\sim}{O}\left( \epsilon^{- {3/2}} \right)$ for finding $\epsilon$-SOSPs.

Finally, in numerical experiments, our method is compared against ARC \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib45 "Adaptive cubic regularisation methods for unconstrained optimization. part i: motivation, convergence and numerical results"), (https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib46 "Adaptive cubic regularisation methods for unconstrained optimization. part ii: worst-case function-and derivative-evaluation complexity")\], the newly proposed TR variant \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib40 "A consistently adaptive trust-region method")\], and the recent gradient regularized Newton method \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib10 "Regularized Newton Method with Global ⁢O(/1k2) Convergence")\], demonstrating the effectiveness of UTR.

## The Adaptive Universal Trust-Region Method

In the above sections, we have provided a concise analysis of the universal trust-region method that applies uniformly to both convex and nonconvex optimization. Nevertheless, the limitation of [Property 2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#S2.Thmproperty2 "Property 2.2 (Function-or-Stationarity-Decrease). ‣ 2.1 Overview of the Method ‣ 2 The Universal Trust-Region Method ‣ Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New AnalysesAccepted by Journal of Scientific Computing, 2026.") lies in its reliance on the unknown Lipschitz constant, which brings the challenge for efficient implementation. To enhance the capability of our method in practice, we provide an adaptive universal trust-region method ([Algorithm 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#algorithm2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 4.1 The Adaptive Framework ‣ 4 The Adaptive Universal Trust-Region Method ‣ Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New AnalysesAccepted by Journal of Scientific Computing, 2026.")), we show that it can preserve the global complexity of the simple strategy and find $\epsilon$-SOSPs. Also, the local convergence can be improved to quadratic.

## Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present numerical experiments. We implement the adaptive UTR ([Algorithm 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#algorithm2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 4.1 The Adaptive Framework ‣ 4 The Adaptive Universal Trust-Region Method ‣ Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New AnalysesAccepted by Journal of Scientific Computing, 2026.")) in Julia programming language. All experiments are performed on a MacBook Pro with Apple M2 Chip and 24GB LPDDR5 Memory. To enable efficient routines for trust-region subproblems, we implement two options. The first option utilizes the standard Cholesky factorization \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib36 "Numerical optimization"), Algorithm 4.3\] and uses a hybrid bisection and Newton method to find the dual variable \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib27 "A New Complexity Result on Minimization of a Quadratic Function with a Sphere Constraint"), (https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib26 "Combining Binary Search and Newton’s Method to Compute Real Roots for a Class of Real Functions")\]. When using this option, we name the method after UTR. The second option is an indirect method (so it is referred to as iUTR) by Krylov subspace iterations, which is consistent with the open-source implementation \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib1 "Scalable adaptive cubic regularization methods")\] of classical trust-region method and adaptive cubic regularized Newton method. Motivated from Curtis and Wang \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib25 "Worst-Case Complexity of TRACE with Inexact Subproblem Solutions for Nonconvex Smooth Optimization")\] and Cartis et al. \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib44 "Evaluation complexity of algorithms for nonconvex optimization: theory, computation and perspectives"), Chapter 10\], we use the Lanczos method with inexactness of subproblem solutions.

### CUTEst benchmarks

We conduct experiments on unconstrained problems with dimension $n \leq 5000$ in the CUTEst benchmarks \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib29 "CUTEst: a Constrained and Unconstrained Testing Environment with safe threads for mathematical optimization")\]. Since many of these problems are nonconvex, we focus on comparisons with the classical trust-region method \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib47 "Trust region methods")\] and adaptive cubic regularized Newton method \[(https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11489v4#bib.bib45 "Adaptive cubic regularisation methods for unconstrained optimization. part i: motivation, convergence and numerical results")\]. All methods use Krylov approaches to solve subproblems. Specifically, the classical trust-region method uses the Steihaug-Toint conjugate gradient method.
