## Introduction

Machine learning methods are at an ever increasing pace being integrated into domains that have classically been within the purview of controls. There is a wide range of examples, including perception-based control, agile robotics, and autonomous driving and racing. As exciting as these developments may be, they have been most pronounced on the experimental and empirical sides. To deploy these systems safely, stably, and robustly into the real world, we argue that a principled and integrated theoretical understanding of a) fundamental limitations and b) statistical optimality is needed. Under the past few years, a host of new techniques have been introduced to our field. Unfortunately, existing results in this area are relatively inaccessible to a typical first or second year graduate student in control theory, as they require both sophisticated mathematical tools not typically included in a control theorist's training (e.g., high-dimensional statistics and learning theory).

This tutorial seeks to provide a streamlined exposition of some of these recent advances that are most relevant to the non-asymptotic theory of linear system identification. Our aim is not to be encyclopedic but rather to give simple proofs of the main developments and to highlight and collect the key technical tools to arrive at these results. For a broader---and less technical---overview of the literature we point the reader to our recent survey (Tsiamis et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib35)). It is also worth to point out that the classical literature on system identification has done a formidable job at---often very accurately---characterizing the asymptotic performance of identification algorithms (Ljung, (https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib17)). Our aim is not to supplant this literature but rather to complement the asymptotic picture with finite sample guarantees by relaying recently developed technical tools drawn from high-dimensional probability, statistics and learning theory (Vershynin, (https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib39); Wainwright, (https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib41)).

The emphasis therein is on anti-concentration---which can hold under milder moment assumptions---rather than concentration. However, the introduction of this tool is not necessary for Gaussian (or sub-Gaussian) system identification. For instance, Sarkar and Rakhlin ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib28)) leverage the method of self-normalized martingales introduced in [Section 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#S4 "4 Self-Normalized Martingale Bounds ‣ A Tutorial on the Non-Asymptotic Theory of System Identification") below.

Our motivation for providing a different proof is to streamline the exposition as to fit control of the lower tail into the \"standard machinery\", which roughly consists of: prove a family of scalar exponential inequalities, invoke the Chernoff method, and conclude by a discretization argument and a union bound to port the result from scalars to matrices. Our proof here follows this outline and emphasizes the exponential inequality in [Theorem 3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#S3.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 3.2. ‣ 3.2 The Lower Tail of the Empirical Covariance of Causal sub-Gaussian Processes ‣ 3 The Lower Spectrum of the Empirical Covariance ‣ A Tutorial on the Non-Asymptotic Theory of System Identification"). We finally remark that the proof presented here is new to the literature and extends a result in Ziemann ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib43)) from the Gaussian setting to the sub-Gaussian setting.

To express results generally and compactly, several definitions are in order.

### Definition 4.1

(Filtration and Adapted Process) A sequence of sub-$\sigma$-algebras $\left\{ \mathcal{F}_{t} \right\}_{t = 1}^{T}$ is said to be a *filtration* if $\mathcal{F}_{t} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{k}$ for $t \leq k$. A stochastic process $\left\{ W_{t} \right\}_{t = 1}^{T}$ is said to be *adapted* to the filtration $\left\{ \mathcal{F}_{t} \right\}_{t = 1}^{T}$ if for all $t \geq 1$, $W_{t}$ is $\mathcal{F}_{t}$-measurable.

Conditioning on a sub-$\sigma$-algebra provides partial information about the total randomness. Therefore, the requirement that a filtration is non-decreasing captures the fact that information is not forgotten. An adapted process is one in which all the randomness at a particular time is explained by the information in the filtration up to that time.

### Definition 4.2

(Martingale) Consider a stochastic process $\left\{ W_{t} \right\}_{t = 1}^{T}$ which is adapted to a filtration $\left\{ \mathcal{F}_{t} \right\}_{t = 1}^{T}$. This process is called a *martingale* if for all $1 \leq t \leq T$, $W_{t}$ is integrable and for all $1 \leq t < T$, ${\mathbf{E}{\lbrack\left. W_{t + 1} \middle| \mathcal{F}_{t} \right.\rbrack}} = W_{t}$.

Martingales model causal or non-anticipative processes. To better appreciate this, note that the increments $W_{t + 1} - W_{t}$ are mean zero and conditionally orthogonal to the past; they can be thought of as the \"next step\" in a random walk whose path is traced out by $W_{t}$.

In the context of the linear time-series model in ([1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#S1.E1 "Equation 1.1 ‣ 1.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ A Tutorial on the Non-Asymptotic Theory of System Identification")), we may define the sub-$\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal{F}_{t}$ as those induced by the randomness up to time $t$: $\mathcal{F}_{t} = {\sigma{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t + 1},V_{1},\ldots,V_{t})}}$. In this case, the process $\left\{ X_{t} \right\}_{t = 1}^{T}$ is adapted to the filtration $\left\{ \mathcal{F}_{t - 1} \right\}_{t = 1}^{T}$ and the process $\left\{ V_{t} \right\}_{t = 1}^{T}$ is adapted to the filtration $\left\{ \mathcal{F}_{t} \right\}_{t = 1}^{T}$.

Dimensional dependence. Ignoring logarithmic terms, the bound implies that the number of samples $T$ should scale linearly with the dimension ${pd_{\mathsf{Y}}} + {qd_{\mathsf{U}}}$ of the covariates $X_{t}$. Since every sample $Y_{t}$ contains at least $d_{\mathsf{Y}}$ measurements, this implies that the total number of measurements should be linear with $d_{\mathsf{Y}} \times {({{pd_{\mathsf{Y}}} + {qd_{\mathsf{U}}}})}$. This scaling is qualitatively correct since $\theta^{\star}$ has $d_{\mathsf{Y}} \times {({{pd_{\mathsf{Y}}} + {qd_{\mathsf{U}}}})}$ unknowns, requiring at least as many independent equations.

Logarithmic dependence on confidence. The error norm scales linearly with $\sqrt{\log{1/\delta}}$. In the asymptotic regime we also recover the same order of $\sqrt{\log{1/\delta}}$ by applying the Central Limit Theorem (CLT). However, in the regime of finite samples, obtaining the rate is non-trivial, see Tsiamis et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib35)), and requires the analysis presented in this tutorial.

System theoretic constants. The identification error is directly affected by the SNR of the system. The more the system is excited and the smaller the noise, the better the SNR becomes. However, excitability varies heavily depending on the system and the choice of excitation policy. In particular, the system's controllability structure can affect the degree of excitation dramatically. Systems with poor controllability structure can exhibit SNR which suffers from curse of dimensionality, i.e., the smallest eigenvalue of $\Sigma_{\tau}$ degrades exponentially with the system dimension Tsiamis and Pappas ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib34)).

The upper bound also increases with the logarithm of the "condition number\" $\det{({\Sigma_{T}\Sigma_{\tau}^{- 1}})}$. For stable systems, this condition number is bounded since $\Sigma_{T}$ converges to a steady-state covariance as $T$ increases; we can neglect it in this case. On the other hand, the term might be significant in the case of general non-explosive systems. Let $\kappa$ be the size of the largest Jordan block of $\mathcal{A}_{11}$ with eigenvalues on the unit circle. Then, this term can be as large as $\kappa{\log T}$.

Burn-in time. The upper bound holds as soon as the number of samples exceeds a "burn-in\" time $T_{\mathsf{p}\mathsf{e}}{(\delta,\tau)}$. If the system is non-explosive, $T_{\mathsf{p}\mathsf{e}}{(\delta,\tau)}$ is always finite for fixed $\tau$. ### Many Trajectories and Finite Hypothesis Classes

In order to make the exposition self-contained, we will now make two simplifying assumptions relating to the finiteness of the hypothesis class and the dependence structure of the covariate process $X_{1:T}$. A more general treatment without these can be found in Ziemann and Tu ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib44)). Here, we impose the following:

The hypothesis class $\mathcal{F}$ is finite; ${|\mathcal{F}|} < \infty$.

We have access to $T/k$-many independent trajectories from the same process: there exists an integer $k \in {\mathbb{N}}$ dividing $T$ such that $X_{1:k},X_{{k + 1}:{2k}},\ldots$ are drawn iid.

We will also impose the following rather minimal integrability condition:

All functions $f \in \mathcal{F}$ are such that ${\mathbf{E}{\|{f{(X_{t})}}\|}_{2}^{4}} < \infty$ for all $t \in {\lbrack T\rbrack}$.

Moreover, as in [Section 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#S5 "5 System Identification ‣ A Tutorial on the Non-Asymptotic Theory of System Identification"), we require the noise to be a sub-Gaussian martingale difference sequence:

For each $t \in {\lbrack T\rbrack}$, $\left. V_{t} \middle| X_{1:t} \right.$ is $\sigma^{2}$ conditionally-sub-Gaussian and mean zero.

Let us now discuss A1-A4. Assumption A1 informs us that the search space for the LSE ([7.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#S7.E2 "Equation 7.2 ‣ 7 Beyond Linear Models ‣ A Tutorial on the Non-Asymptotic Theory of System Identification")) is finite. This is mainly imposed to avoid the introduction of the chaining technique which is the standard alternative to the bounds from [Section 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#S4 "4 Self-Normalized Martingale Bounds ‣ A Tutorial on the Non-Asymptotic Theory of System Identification"). Using this technique, similar statements can for instance be derived for compact subsets of bounded function classes (Ziemann and Tu, (https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib44)). Assumption A2 controls the dependence structure of the process. Here, we assume that we are able to restart the process every $k$ time steps. Again, a more general statement relying on stochastic stability can be found in Ziemann and Tu ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib44)). Assumption A3 is relatively standard. Arguably the strongest assumption is A4, which in principle yields that the conditional expectation (given all past data) is a function in the search space $\mathcal{F}$. It is a so-called realizability assumption---the model ([7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#S7.E1 "Equation 7.1 ‣ 7 Beyond Linear Models ‣ A Tutorial on the Non-Asymptotic Theory of System Identification")) is well-specified---and it is not currently known how to remove it and still obtain sharp bounds beyond linear classes (for an analysis of linear misspecified models, see Ziemann et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib45)).

### Notes

As noted in the previous section, the idea of using the "offset" basic inequality relied on here is due to Rakhlin and Sridharan ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib24)); Liang et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib16)). The "many trajectores"-style of analysis used here is due to Tu et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib36)) who introduced it in the linear setting. Here, we have extended their style of analysis to simplify the exposition of Ziemann and Tu ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib44)) who consider the single trajectory setting, but rely on a rather more advanced exponential inequality due to Samson ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib27)). Alternatively, one can also easily extend the lower uniform law in [Proposition F.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#A6.Thmproposition1 "Proposition F.1. ‣ The Lower Tail Revisited ‣ F.2 Proofs for Nonlinear Identification ‣ Appendix F Proofs for Section 6 and Section 7 ‣ A Tutorial on the Non-Asymptotic Theory of System Identification") to certain classes of mixing processes by invoking the blocking technique of Yu ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib42)) combined with a truncation style of argument such as that used in the proof of Theorem 14.12 of Wainwright ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib41)), see also Ziemann et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib45), the proof of Theorem 4.3). Note however, that all the analyses above and in this section necessitate some degree of stability (mixing). This should be contrasted with the system identification bounds of [Section 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#S5 "5 System Identification ‣ A Tutorial on the Non-Asymptotic Theory of System Identification"), which work even in the marginally regime. In principle, the consequence of this is that while the convergence rates for bounds such as [Theorem 7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#S7.Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 7.1. ‣ 7.1 Many Trajectories and Finite Hypothesis Classes ‣ 7 Beyond Linear Models ‣ A Tutorial on the Non-Asymptotic Theory of System Identification") are correct, the burn-ins are deflated by various dependency measures.

There have also been other, more algorithmically focused, approaches to nonlinear identification problems in the recent literature. Noteably, gradient based methods in generalized linear models of the form $X_{t + 1} = {{\phi{({A^{\star}X_{t}})}} + V_{t}}$ (with $\phi$ a known nonlinearity) have been the topic of a number of recent papers (see e.g. Foster et al., (https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib7); Sattar and Oymak, (https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib30)). The sharpest bounds for parameter recovery in this setting are due to Kowshik et al. ((https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03873v2#bib.bib12)).
