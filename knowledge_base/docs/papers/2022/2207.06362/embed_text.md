Problems of the form ((https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#S1.E1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Iterative Linear Quadratic Optimization for Nonlinear Control: Differentiable Programming Algorithmic Templates")) have been tackled in various ways, from direct approaches using nonlinear optimization (Betts (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib7); Wright (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib61); [Wright, 1991a,](https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib62); Wright (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib64); Pantoja (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib42); Dunn and Bertsekas (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib16); Rao et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib45)) to convex relaxations using semi-definite optimization (Boyd and Vandenberghe (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib10)). Numerous packages exist for such problems such as CasAdi (Andersson et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib2)), Pyomo (Bynum et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib13)), JumP (Dunning et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib17)), IPOPT (Wächter and Biegler (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib59)), or SNOPT (Gill et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib22)), Crocoddyl (Jallet et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib27)), acados (Verschueren et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib56)). A popular approach of the former category proceeds by computing at each iteration the linear quadratic regulator associated with a linear quadratic approximation of the problem around the current candidate solutions (Jacobson and Mayne (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib26); Li and Todorov (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib30); Sideris and Bobrow (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib50); Tassa et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib53)). The computed feedback policies are then applied either along the linearized dynamics or along the original dynamics to output a new candidate solution. Such canonical nonlinear control algorithms efficiently incorporate second-order information into the optimization procedure by exploiting the dynamical structure of the problem. This approach lends itself to an integration in a differentiable programming framework to extend this paradigm beyond first-order oracles.

Differentiable programming consists of the implementation of functions in a programming language that enables access to derivatives of these functions by automatic differentiation (Baur and Strassen (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib4); Rumelhart et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib48); LeCun (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib29); Schmidhuber (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib49); Gilbert (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib21); Werbos (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib60); Griewank and Walther (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib24); Baydin et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib5); Bolte and Pauwels (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib9); Abadi et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib1); Paszke et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib43)). Automatic differentiation itself has roots in the control literature, and its use is pervasive in numerous domains (Griewank and Walther (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib24)), in particular deep learning (Zhang et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib65); Goodfellow et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib23)). Canonical nonlinear control algorithms incorporating second order information can also be integrated in reinforcement learning pipelines (Recht (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib46); Kakade et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib28)), and may then benefit from a differentiable programming viewpoint to isolate their underlying principles. These algorithms have indeed generally be presented through linear algebraic manipulations instantiated separately for each algorithm, which hinder a global perspective (Murray and Yakowitz (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib38); Pantoja (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib42); Li and Todorov (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib30); Sideris and Bobrow (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib50); Tassa et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib53)).

The motivation of this work is to cast all such algorithms in a common differentiable programming viewpoint to delineate the discrepancies between the different algorithms and identify the common subroutines. We review the implementation of (i) a Gauss-Newton method (Sideris and Bobrow (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib50)), a.k.a. Iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (ILQR), (ii) a Newton method (Pantoja (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib42); Liao and Shoemaker (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib31); Dunn and Bertsekas (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib16)), (iii) a differential dynamic programming approach based on linear approximations of the dynamics and quadratic approximations of the costs, a.k.a. iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (iLQR) (Tassa et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib53)), (iv) a differential dynamic programming approach based on quadratic approximations of both dynamics and costs, usually simply called DDP (Jacobson and Mayne (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#bib.bib26)), and consider regularized variants of the aforementioned algorithms with their corresponding line searches. In turn, the differentiable programming viewpoint informs efficient handling of memory by appropriate check-pointing. An extended related work discussion is in Appendix [B](https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#A2 "Annexe B Related Work ‣ Annexe A Tensor Notation ‣ 5 Computational Complexity ‣ Iterative Linear Quadratic Optimization for Nonlinear Control: Differentiable Programming Algorithmic Templates").\

Outline. In Sec. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#S2 "2 From Linear Quadratic Control Problem to Nonlinear Control Algorithm ‣ Iterative Linear Quadratic Optimization for Nonlinear Control: Differentiable Programming Algorithmic Templates") we recall how linear quadratic control problems are solved by dynamic programming and used as a building block for nonlinear control algorithms. The implementation of classical optimization oracles such as a gradient step, a Gauss-Newton step, or a Newton step is presented in Sec. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#S3 "3 Classical Optimization Oracle ‣ Iterative Linear Quadratic Optimization for Nonlinear Control: Differentiable Programming Algorithmic Templates"). Sec. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#S4 "4 Differential Dynamic Programming Oracle ‣ Iterative Linear Quadratic Optimization for Nonlinear Control: Differentiable Programming Algorithmic Templates") details the rationale and implementation of differential dynamic programming approaches. Sec. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#S5 "5 Computational Complexity ‣ Iterative Linear Quadratic Optimization for Nonlinear Control: Differentiable Programming Algorithmic Templates") presents the computational complexities of each oracle in terms of space and time complexities in a differentiable programming framework. All algorithms are tested on several synthetic problems in Sec. (https://arxiv.org/html/2207.06362v2#S6 "6 Experiments ‣ 5 Computational Complexity ‣ Iterative Linear Quadratic Optimization for Nonlinear Control: Differentiable Programming Algorithmic Templates"): swinging-up a fixed pendulum, and autonomous car racing with simple dynamics.
