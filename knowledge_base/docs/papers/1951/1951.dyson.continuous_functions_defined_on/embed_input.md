Continuous Functions Defined on Spheres

Topics include Algebraic topology, Borsuk-Ulam theorem, Sphere theorems, Continuous functions, Coincidence theorems, Inscribed square, Topological combinatorics.

<!-- chunk {"id": "summary-0002", "role": "summary", "section": "Summary", "weight": 2.0} -->

Dyson proves a Borsuk-Ulam-style theorem for continuous real-valued functions on the 2-sphere: there are four points on the sphere, forming the vertices of a square centered at the sphere's center, at which the function takes the same value. Equivalently, the theorem finds two perpendicular diameters of the sphere whose four endpoints share a common function value. The result sits in the line of Kakutani and Yamabe-Yujobo sphere theorems used in geometric existence problems, and Dyson's proof adapts their topological method through reflection and connectedness arguments rather than giving a constructive way to locate the square. The paper is also historically useful because it identifies the natural higher-dimensional conjecture, equal values on the endpoints of several mutually perpendicular diameters, while leaving that extension to later work by Yang.

The following theorem has recently been proved by Yamabe and Yujobo. Let f(x) be a real-valued continuous function defined on the surface S of an n-dimensional sphere with center Z in Euclidean (n + 1)-dimensional space R^{n+1}. Then there exist (n + 1) points x_0, x_1,..., x_n on S such that the vectors Zx_0,..., Zx_n are orthogonal and f(x_0) = f(x_1) =... = f(x_n). This theorem was conjectured some years ago by Kakutani. He proved the case n = 2, generally known as Kakutani's theorem. The object of the present paper is to prove the following analog of Kakutani's theorem. Theorem. Let S be the surface of a sphere center Z in Euclidean 3-space R^3, and let f(x) be a continuous real-valued function defined on S.

Then there exist four points x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4 on S forming the vertices of a square with center Z, such that f(x_1) = f(x_2) = f(x_3) = f(x_4). This theorem is stronger than Kakutani's theorem in that it requires 4 equal values of the function instead of 3. On the other hand, neither theorem is contained in the other. Presumably they will finally appear as two special cases of a more general theorem.
